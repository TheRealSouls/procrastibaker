# **Kavanagh Quotes**

# Inniskeen Road: July Evening (1936)

| Quote | Poetic Technique | Meaning |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| “The bicycles go by in twos and threes” | AlliterationImagery | Tandem bikes were in fashion during Kavanagh’s time, however they are not relevant today. Starts off the poem by creating a playful musicality through alliteration. |
| “There’s a dance in Billy Brennan’s barn tonight” | Alliteration | Kavanagh rhythmically describes the hustle and bustle through his extensive use of alliteration. |
| “And there’s the half-talk code of mysteries / And the wink-and-elbow language of delight” | Imagery Enjambment | Kavanagh refers to the presence of flirting and other non-verbal cues to convey the exuberance in the barn. The enjambment here mirrors the sense of hustle and bustle of the people on their way to “Billy Brennan’s barn”. |
| “Not / A footfall tapping secrecies of stone” | Imagery Sibilance Personification | He describes the absence of footsteps along the road through sibilance, emphasizing the silence. |
| “I have what every poet hates in spite / Of all the solemn talk of contemplation” | Irony Volta | There is irony after the volta in the sonnet. Despite the romanticised view of poetic silence, Kavanagh yearns for the company of like-minded society and the company of kindred spirits.  |
| “Oh, Alexander Selkirk knew the plight / Of being king and government and nation” | Allusion Polysyndeton | Alexander Selkirk was a voluntary castaway in the Pacific Ocean. He relates to him, portraying his existence as that of an island, surrounded by water all around. The polysyndeton conveys the abundance of his status. |
| “A road, a mile of kingdom, I am king / Of banks and stones and every blooming thing” | Polysyndeton Pun | Every blooming thing could be viewed as the growing of plants, but it could also be interpreted as a mild curse. Given his alienation, I would interpret this as negative. |

**Excitement of the dance:** hustle and bustle, exuberance, flurry of activity, teeming with people, commotion  
**Kavanagh’s sense of loneliness:** alienation, estrangement, seclusion, desolation, describing himself as the solitary sentinel of the island that Selkirk was on

# Shancoduff (1937)

| Quote | Poetic Technique | Meaning |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| “My black hills have never seen the sun rising” | Imagery Sibilance Personification | The image of “black” elucidates that his land is infertile. They face away from the sun, and the hills have never “seen” the sunrise. |
| “Lot’s wife would not be salt if she had been / Incurious as my black hills that are happy” | Allusion Simile Metaphor | Lot and his family were told not to look back on the destruction of Sodom in the Bible else they would get turned to salt. Despite this, Lot’s wife’s curiosity got the better of her \- she looked back and got turned to ashes. This metaphor illustrates how inconspicuous his patch of land is. |
| “My hills hoard the bright shillings of March / While the sun searches in every pocket” | Alliteration Pun Sibilance Personification | Kavanagh makes his hills an active participant in the poem. “Hoard the bright shillings of March” is a pun and could be interpreted in two ways \- he could be referring to clearings or economic gain. The sibilance of “sun searches” conveys the lack of sunlight for the land, highlighting the sun’s unapologetic nature. |
| “They are my Alps and I have climbed the Matterhorn” | Metaphor Hyperbole Allusion | Despite people’s comments, Kavanagh loves the land and directly compares it to the “Alps” and how he has climbed the “Matterhorn”. Kavanagh is clearly being playful with this hyperbolic metaphor, but this highlights his love for the land. |
| “The sleety winds fondle the rushy beards of Shancoduff” | Personification | Kavanagh makes the hills active participants in the poem, allowing his readers to emotionally connect with his patch of land. |
| “I hear and is my heart not badly shaken?” | Rhyme (the only) Imagery | Kavanagh retaliates against the “cattle-drovers” who scorn at his patch of infertile land, elucidating his emotional connection to Shancoduff. The words “forsaken” and “shaken” form the only rhyme in this poem, ending it with a perfect cadence and leaving his rebuttal finished. |

**The dullness of Shancoduff:** drab monotony, Kavanagh turns a seemingly inert landscape into a haven cherished for its simplicity, latent vitality of the land, banal, inconspicuous patch of land  
**Kavanagh’s delight in the land:** reverence, Kavanagh’s haven/oasis, Kavanagh’s veneration for the land  
**The rash comments of the “cattle-drovers”:** bluntness, derisive remarks, derision, Kavanagh refutes/retorts this callousness

# A Christmas Childhood (1940 / 1943\)

| Quote | Poetic Technique | Meaning |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| “One side of the potato-pits was white with frost” | Alliteration Sibilance | Kavanagh embellishes his poem with alliteration interwoven with sibilance |
| “How wonderful that was, how wonderful\!” | Repetition Exclamation  | Repetition of “wonderful” emphasizes his delightful childhood experiences of the Christmas season. |
| “The light between the ricks of hay and straw / Was a hole in Heaven’s gable” | Metaphor Imagery Alliteration | Kavanagh utilises biblical metaphors to detail his positive experiences with the world around him as a child. |
| “An apple tree / With its December-glinting fruit we saw \- / O you, Eve, were the world that tempted me.” | Allusion  | He uses a biblical allusion to lament the temptation of the outside world that lured him away from this childhood place of innocence and contentment. |
| “Any common sight… \[was\] / Of a beauty that the world did not touch.” | Hyperbole | Kavanagh reminisces on how during childhood, everything was a magnificent sight to him, even the ordinary ones. |
| “My father played the melodion / Outside at our gate; / There were stars in the morning east / And they danced to his music” | Imagery | To young Kavanagh’s delighted eyes, even the stars were dancing to his father’s melodies. |
| “Outside in the cow-house my mother / Made the music of milking.” | Imagery Alliteration | Kavanagh’s mother attended to the more prosaic task of milking cows. Yet, to the child’s ears, the rhythmic patter of the milk being squeezed into the bucket is a kind of bass accompaniment to his father’s melodion. |
| “A water-hen screeched in the bog, / Mass-going feet / Crunched the wafer-ice on the pot-holes / Somebody wistfully twisted the bellows wheel.” | Onomatopoeia Imagery Alliteration | The onomatopoeia embellishes the poem with sound and appeals to our sense of hearing. |
| “My child poet picked out the letters / On the grey stone” | Alliteration  | Even as a child, the letters and words on a tombstone were fascinating to young Kavanagh, and he conveys this excitement through his use of alliteration. |
| “I nicked six nicks on the door-post / With my pen-knife’s big blade \- / There was a little one for cutting tobacco / And I was six Christmases of age” | Alliteration Sibilance | The alliteration creates a concerto of childhood delight here. There is a nostalgic and symbolic declaration in the line, “I was six Christmases of age” |
| “Cassiopeia was over / Cassidy’s hanging hill” | Alliteration Imagery Allusion Colloquialism | This image shows us how magic appears in the ordinary, this time over the hill of Kavanagh’s neighbour named Cassidy. This juxtaposition conveys the childhood sense that anything is possible. |
| “I had a prayer like a white rose pinned / On the Virgin Mary’s blouse” | Imagery Allusion Symbolism | Symbolises Kavanagh’s devotion to the Christian religion, with a white rose symbolising purity, at a time before he violently at |

# 

**Kavanagh’s childhood wonder:** his untarnished perception, outlook enchanted by biblical stories, small visual miracles described as “of a beauty that the world did not touch”  
**Sounds in the poem:** concerto of childhood delight, Kavanagh turns mundane activities wondrous through sound, mother milking adding a rhythmic patter, accordion is a tenor accompaniment  
**Kavanagh’s familial bond:** emphasizes the sanctity found in daily life and familial bonds, meaningful attachment, represents continuity in Kavanagh’s fractured world

# Canal Bank Walk (1960)

| Quote | Poetic Technique | Meaning |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| “Leafy-with-love banks and the green waters of the canal / Pouring redemption for me” | Alliteration Neologism Personification | Kavanagh personifies pieces of nature such as the canal pouring redemption for him. “Leafy-with-love” is a creative and playful syllogism to describe nature. “Pouring redemption” means that nature heals his soul. |
| “Wallow in the habitual, the banal” | Imagery | Kavanagh encourages us to immerse ourselves in the ordinary to heal our soul. |
| “The couple kissing on an old seat / And a bird gathering materials for the nest for the Word” | Imagery Internal rhyme | Back when Catholicism stigmatised sexuality in Ireland, Kavanagh elucidates its beauty and cherishes its sights. |
| “Eloquently new and abandoned to its delirious beat” | Pun | “Delirious” could mean either ecstatic and excited or utterly unorthodox due to the stigmas surrounding sexuality in Catholic Ireland. |
| “O unworn world, enrapture me, encapture me in a web” | Internal rhyme Apostrophe Personification Parallelism | In Kavanagh’s apostrophe, he urges nature to entangle him in its beauty and heal his soul after moving from Monaghan. The internal rhyme adds a melodic accompaniment to this line. |
| “Of fabulous grass and eternal voices by a beech, / Feed the gaping need of my senses, give me ad lib” | Assonance Synaesthesia | “Fabulous grass” is synaesthetic imagery here in the sense that fabulous appeals to our sense of sight and grass appeals to our sense of touch. This blending of sensory experience fabricates a vivid poem. The repetition of the “ee” sound gives a beat to the enjambment. |
| “For this soul needs to be honoured with a new dress woven / From green and blue things and arguments that cannot be proven” | Imagery Metaphor Juxtaposition | Kavanagh wants to recreate himself using nature. He cannot prove this healing power, deeming it “an argument that cannot be proven”. |

**Nature’s healing power:** irrefutable power, Kavanagh’s idea of a new eden, rebirth, restorative power, cleansing stillness, reprieve from cognitive noise  
**Beauty of love:** emotional attunement, harmony, Kavanagh romanticises intimacy in a Catholic world

# Lines Written on a Seat on the Grand Canal, Dublin (1960)

| Quote | Poetic Technique | Meaning |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| “O commemorate me where there is water, / Canal water preferably” | Apostrophe Imagery | Kavanagh asks to be commemorated near a body of water. |
| “So stilly, / Greeny at the heart of summer” | Neologism Imagery | Kavanagh’s neologisms are playful and creative here to describe the water. |
| “Brother / Commemorate me thus beautifully” | Repetition Direct address | Kavanagh’s asking his younger brother, Peter, to commemorate him wholeheartedly. |
| “Where by a lock Niagarously roars” | Neologism Imagery Onomatopoeia Metaphor | Kavanagh’s wonderful neologism describes the water of the canal as “niagariously”, thus cleverly comparing it to the Niagara Falls in North America. |
| “No one will speak in prose / Who finds his way to these Parnassian islands” | Allusion Metaphor | Mount Parnassus was the home of poetry in Greek mythology. Kavanagh is hence saying that you will speak in poetry and ascertain the beauty of ordinary life once you sit on the bench. |
| “And look\! a barge comes bringing from Athy” | Exclamation Caesura Alliteration | This pause exclaims Kavanagh’s wonder to see a boat from Athy. It was 80 kilometres away from Dublin, but that was quite a distance back then. |
| “O commemorate me with no hero-courageous / Tomb \- just a canal-bank seat for the passer-by” | Apostrophe Allusion | Kavanagh may have been slyly digging at Yeats’ instructions for burial in his poem, ‘Under Ben Bulben’. In contrast, Kavanagh’s instructions are modest, yet mocking at the same time. |

**Kavanagh’s desire for legacy:** endurance, endurance through presence rather than reputation, contentment as final achievement  
**Description of the bench:** enlivened canal through imagery and neologisms, sibilance emphasizes the hush and quiet
